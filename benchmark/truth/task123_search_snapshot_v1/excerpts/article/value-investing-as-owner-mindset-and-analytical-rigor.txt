
In his 2006 Columbia Business School address, [[li-lu|Li Lu]] synthesizes the framework that transforms [[value-investing|value investing]] from a theoretical concept into an actionable strategy for outsized gains. The core argument rests on four interconnected principles: adopting a strict business owner mindset, exploiting structural market inefficiencies created by the short-term majority, conducting primary investigative research, and recognizing rare high-conviction insights.

First, [[li-lu|Li Lu]] redefines the investor as a business owner rather than a security trader. Because minority owners lack operational control, requiring a substantial [[margin-of-safety|margin of safety]] is an essential act of self-defense. This mindset decouples an investor's decisions from market sentiment, framing price drops not as losses but as opportunities to evaluate tangible balance-sheet assets like liquid working capital, physical real estate, or cash-generating subsidiaries.

Second, [[li-lu|Li Lu]] highlights the structural divide in financial markets: approximately 95% of institutional capital prioritizes short-term trading metrics, leaving roughly 5% governed by true value principles. This institutional imperative causes the majority to abandon solid businesses facing transient bad news, legal noise, or illiquidity. Value investors exploit these dislocations using Graham's [[mr-market|Mr. Market]] paradigm, treating market price swings as emotional counter-offers rather than fundamental facts.

Third, uncovering true mispricings requires investigative research comparable to academic journalism. In case studies like Timberland and Hyundai Department Store, [[li-lu|Li Lu]] outlines an exhaustive due diligence process—downloading court filings, evaluating hidden affiliate assets, interviewing local community members, and inspecting physical properties. This primary work eliminates qualitative uncertainty, giving the investor the fortitude to place concentrated bets when markets panic.
