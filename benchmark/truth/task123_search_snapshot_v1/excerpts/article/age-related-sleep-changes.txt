
As the human body ages, fundamental shifts occur across [[sleep-architecture]], neurohormonal regulation, and circadian alignment. While older adults maintain a constant physiological requirement for seven to eight hours of sleep per night, age-related brain atrophy, pineal calcification, and structural tissue laxity impair the ability to achieve continuous, high-quality rest.

Changes in [[sleep-architecture]] include reduced total sleep time, increased nocturnal wakefulness, and a dramatic decline in deep delta sleep—a stage essential for physical recovery and spatial [[memory-consolidation]]. Concurrently, degradation of the suprachiasmatic nucleus and decreased environmental light exposure alter [[circadian-rhythm]] synchronization, frequently precipitating [[advanced-sleep-phase-syndrome]].

Beyond normal physiological decline, aging significantly increases susceptibility to specific sleep pathologies. Upper airway collapse leads to high rates of [[obstructive-sleep-apnea]], while psychological and behavioral reactions to sleep loss frequently develop into chronic [[insomnia]]. In older men, neurodegenerative changes can manifest as [[rem-sleep-behavior-disorder]], where muscle paralysis during [[rem-sleep]] fails. Management across all age-related sleep disorders emphasizes non-pharmacological interventions, including morning bright-light exposure, proper [[sleep-hygiene]], and specialized modalities like [[cognitive-behavioral-therapy-for-insomnia]].
